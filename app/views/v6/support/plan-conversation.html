Use this guide to help you plan a conversation with your child's other parent or other involved people. Only continue with the conversation if it is safe. If you need specialised support you can find a mediator (opens in a new tab).

Step 1: Decide what you need to talk about

When planning a conversation, you should:

  • clarify the purpose
  • decide the outcome you are hoping for
  • consider what information you can and cannot share

It can be helpful to keep your child at the centre of the conversation so you both can focus on the goal of doing what's best for them.

Step 2: Decide how, where and when to talk

Think about somewhere to meet where you will both feel able to talk to each other. Depending on where you feel comfortable, you could meet at:

  • one parent's home
  • a friend or family member's home
  • a public place (for example, a cafe or library)

If meeting face to face will not work, think about other forms of communication (for example, text, email or telephone).

Try to agree on a day and time when you can talk openly, without children around. Leave yourself plenty of time so you are not under pressure.

Step 3: Decide if you want other people to be there

You could ask a friend or family member to be there during your conversation. Some people find this helpful if it is a person both parents trust and who can stay calm and neutral. If you want someone else to be with you, make sure you tell the other parent so they know what to expect.

Step 4: Decide next actions

It can be helpful to write your responses to these discussion points so you can remember them or share them with the other parent. You can also talk through your plans with someone else to help you prepare.

Advice for conversations

If your relationship with your child's other parent is difficult or complicated, it is often best to keep your child as the focus of conversations. Here are some strategies to help you work together.

Asking for a conversation

When planning or setting up a conversation, you should:

  • Clarify the purpose
  • Decide the outcome you are hoping for
  • Consider what information you can and cannot share
  • Avoid starting the conversation when you or the other parent is in a hurry, tired, or stressed

Avoiding conflict

To lower the chance of conflict, you can:

  • Start with things you have in common, even if they are not your top priority
  • Be polite and treat the other parent as you would want to be treated
  • Use "I" statements like "I feel concerned when..." instead of accusations like "you never..." or "you always..."
  • Talk about specific examples instead of generalisations

Experiencing conflict

It may be best to return to the conversation later if there is too much conflict. However, if you choose to continue you can try to:

  • Understand and acknowledge the other person's feelings
  • Acknowledge your own mistakes
  • Take responsibility for your contributions
  • Be clear about your needs and limits
  • Talk about consequences as boundaries instead of threats

Ending the conversation

At the end of a conversation, it can be helpful to:

  • Summarise what you talked about to make sure you both have the same understanding
  • Confirm decisions and next steps (possibly in writing)
  • Thank the other person for engaging
